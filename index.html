            What each fight asks for

            A build is not good or bad on its own — it is good or bad against something, and these are the somethings. The bars show how much each fight cares about each axis, relative to the others. The threshold is how much it takes to beat: it climbs the whole way down, which is why a build that walks the Basement can still lose the run four floors later.

            Where the weights came from

            These are the most opinionated numbers in the project and the least sourced — nothing in the game publishes "Mom's Heart cares about tracking". They are a reading of what each fight actually punishes: a stationary target with a huge health pool is a damage check, a room full of fast small enemies is an area check, a boss that teleports is a tracking check.

            They are also the numbers most worth arguing with. Everything downstream — the difficulty solve, every score the site has ever shown you — rests on them, so if one is wrong it is wrong everywhere. data/bosses.json is where they live and CONTRIBUTING.md is how to argue.

            How the number is made.

            Every item carries two layers. What the game says about it, and what it actually feels like to fight with. Only the second one can be argued about, and it is the only one written by hand.

            Two layers

            scraped comes out of items.xml and itempools.xml — quality, pools, item type, heart containers. It is never typed from memory, because a plausible-looking wrong quality number is worse than no quality number at all.

            rated is hand-assigned combat effectiveness across five axes. This is the part that cannot be scraped, and it is where the game's feel lives.

            The five axes

            AxisRangeMeaning
            offense0.5 – 3.0Effective DPS against one large target. 1.0 is neutral.
            aoe0.5 – 3.0Damage against adds and multiple targets. Drives the Beast fight.
            tracking0.0 – 1.0How reliably the build lands hits on an erratic, teleporting target. Drives Delirium.
            defense0.8 – 2.5Effective HP: containers, damage reduction, shields, on-hit heals.
            evasion0.0 – 1.0Mobility: flight, speed, range.

            A completely neutral item is 1.0 / 1.0 / 0.0 / 1.0 / 0.0. Sad Onion is the calibration anchor at 1.22 offense — if an item feels stronger than Sad Onion in a real run, its offense should exceed 1.22, and the gap should be proportional to how much stronger.

            Composing five items into one build

            • offense — multiply all five, then soft-cap at min(p, 3.5 + 0.4 × (p − 3.5)). Stops two Q4 damage items producing an auto-win.
            • aoe — multiply, same soft cap.
            • defense — multiply, hard cap at 4.0.
            • tracking — max() of the five. Homing does not stack; you either have it or you do not.
            • evasion — diminishing: 1 − Π(1 − eᵢ).

            How items interact

            The rules above treat five items as five independent multipliers, which is not how a build works. Homing turns a laser from a charge you waste on a blinking boss into the thing that kills it. A familiar booster is dead weight until there is a familiar to boost. Two items that both want to replace your tears mostly cancel, because one simply overwrites the other.

            So a second pass runs over the finished set. Each rule states a condition over the whole build — these tags present, this tag on three separate items, this tag absent — and an effect. Multiplicative axes multiply; tracking and evasion state a level rather than a scaling, so a synergy raises them to a floor and a conflict caps them.

            The composition ceilings are re-applied afterwards. A synergy can push a build up, but not past the soft cap, or the soft cap would stop meaning anything.

            RuleFires whenEffect

            Because rules read the whole build rather than pairs, the draft stops being “take the biggest number”. During a run each candidate shows what it would combine with, so a weaker item that fits can beat a stronger one that does not.

            Chaos

            Every roll here is a Pool × Quality intersection, and the pool half is the whole constraint — it is what stops a Treasure Room roll from offering you the Devil Room’s best item. Chaos removes it. The game’s own description of the item is “all item pools are combined”, so once you hold it every roll is quality alone and every item of that quality is on the table.

            That makes it the sharpest decision in the draft, because Chaos does nothing in a fight. It is a pick spent on nothing, paid now, for a better draw on every pick after it — so its value is almost entirely a question of when it turns up. Simulated over four hundred drafts a side, playing as well as the offers allow:

            ChaosMedianTop 10%
            never taken22.6%45.9%
            taken on pick 134.2%56.8%
            taken on pick 229.9%52.4%
            taken on pick 419.6%42.4%

            Early it is close to a free upgrade to the rest of the run. On the fourth pick it is one wasted slot and one improved one, which is a loss. Nothing had to be tuned to make that true — it falls out of the item costing a pick and paying only forwards.

            It is rare enough to stay a treat rather than a strategy: it sits at quality 3 in the Shop and Secret Room, which works out at a 0.64% chance of appearing in any given roll and 3.2% of turning up at all across a five-pick draft.

            The daily never offers it. All five of its rounds are dealt before the first pick, so that everybody answers the same question — which means an item whose entire effect is on later rolls has no effect there at all, and offering one it cannot honour would be worse than leaving it out.

            Playing it

            The number keys take the item in that position, R restarts, and P and Q spend a respin on the pool or the quality. Clicking an item’s name or sprite opens everything the site knows about it — its five axes, which rules it can trigger, which families it counts toward, and which rolls can even offer it — without spending your pick on it.

            A run can also be seeded. #/draft?seed=anything deals from that seed alone, so a link is a challenge rather than a description of one: whoever opens it gets exactly the deal you got. It is the same machinery the daily runs on, pointed at a string of your choosing instead of the date.

            Endless

            The five-pick draft asks how good a build you can assemble blind. Endless asks whether your build can grow faster than the ladder climbs: take one item, face one fight, and if you clear it, draft again with what you have.

            That inverts the tension. In a five-pick draft a weak early item is a wasted slot you make up for later. In Endless it is a fight you might not survive to regret, and an item that pays off in four rounds is worth nothing if round two kills you.

            The thirteen real fights come first, in order and untouched, so an Endless run opens on the real game. Past them the ladder keeps climbing: the same fights come round again, and how far above the real ladder they sit grows with the square of how deep you are, so no build can outrun it and no run can go on forever. The first attempt used a step per lap and put a wall exactly where the reward should be — twelve fights cleared comfortably, then one four whole thresholds higher. The climb now starts from nothing at the boundary and builds, which reads on screen as a long cruise and then a fast ending, with the per-fight odds sliding visibly for several fights before the one that gets you.

            Each fight is rolled at exactly the probability the ladder shows. No pity is applied and none is withheld.

            The item pool does not deplete here, which is the difference between a long run and an endless one. A five-pick draft takes an item out of the pool once you have it, because being offered something you already hold is a wasted option; a run that can outlast the pool cannot afford that. Sixty-eight of the ninety-nine intersections hold fewer than twelve items and six hold exactly one, so with depletion a long run slowly strips its own board — a hundred and twenty picks left seventy-seven cells still holding anything. Without it the board is the same on the hundredth fight as on the first.

            Duplicates stack the way they do in the game: two of the same tear up really are two tear ups. Only the stat side stacks, though. Tags are counted by distinct item, so three copies of one Guppy item are not a Guppy — a family still needs three different things carrying the tag.

            The daily challenge

            One puzzle a day, the same one for everybody, built from the date alone. There is no server behind this page, so nothing can hand a puzzle out — instead the day seeds a generator and the generator deals the rounds, which means two people on opposite sides of the world derive the same five offers independently. The day rolls over at midnight UTC rather than locally, or players in different time zones would spend most of their overlapping hours on different puzzles.

            Two rules make a daily run comparable in a way an ordinary one is not.

            All five rounds are dealt before you pick anything. In free play the next roll is drawn after you choose, so two players who pick differently see different offers from round two onward and stop answering the same question. Here everyone sees the same thirty items in the same five groups, and the only thing that varies is what they take.

            There are no respins. A respin is a fresh draw, which would put two players on different puzzles immediately. The daily asks something narrower than free play does — given exactly these five offers, what is the best build you can make — and that question has one answer everybody is chasing.

            The daily runs the Casual ruleset. Advanced draws from a smaller pool and answers to its own difficulty solve, so a daily that sometimes ran one and sometimes the other would not be comparable between two players, let alone between two days.

            A round is drawn only from intersections holding at least four items. A one-item cell in free play is texture, and the game says so out loud; as a fifth of a fixed puzzle everyone is measured on, it is a round nobody gets to play.

            How much of the deal you found

            Because a daily fixes all five offers before the first pick, it has an exact best answer — and without one, a score says very little. 24% sounds respectable and is dreadful if the deal allowed 54%; 12% sounds poor and is close to perfect if 13% was the ceiling. So every possible build is enumerated when a daily finishes and you are told the share of them you beat. A deal allows a few thousand builds, which is about a tenth of a second of brute force rather than a search that might miss the answer. Free play cannot do this: it redraws after every pick, so its tree is the whole item pool deep and there is nothing to exhaust.

            The first attempt interpolated between the deal’s floor and its ceiling, and flattered everybody — the worst build in a deal is often a thousandth of a percent, which made scoring 2% out of a possible 54% read as “the better half”. A percentile needs no scale chosen for it and answers the question directly.

            The best line itself stays hidden until you ask for it. It is worth having and worth not being shown before you have decided you want it.

            Coming back

            Streaks and a record of past days are kept, bucketed by the share of each deal you beat rather than by raw score — raw scores are not comparable between days, because 12% on a deal that allowed 13% is a better day’s work than 30% on one that allowed 60%. A streak survives an unplayed today and only breaks on a missed day; ending it the moment you woke up would be a punishment for sleeping. All of it lives in your browser, so there is no leaderboard and no account, and clearing site data ends the streak.

            Sharing a result posts the shape of the run — the quality of each pick, what it came to, and the ceiling it was measured against — and never an item name, so posting it does not hand the answer to somebody who has not played yet.

            What an item row says

            181 items were rated by hand and carry a note written at the same time. The other 512 a draft can offer needed something better than the raw tag list they used to print, so a row now answers in the best terms available to it, in this order:

            1. the hand-rated note, where one exists;
            2. a hand-written verdict on whether the item is worth a pick — these exist for the items a player is offered most often;
            3. a sourced one-line description of what the item actually does;
            4. a sentence built from its tags, for the stat-ups that need no prose.

            Two and three answer different questions on purpose. What an item does is a fact about the game; whether to take it is a judgement about this game, and a five-item draft judged on thirteen boss fights is unkind to most of Isaac. Pills, trinkets, coins, keys and extra rooms are a large share of what the game hands you and almost none of it kills anything, so those notes say so plainly rather than letting a player wonder why the most exciting item in the offer scored nothing.

            Nothing in either layer is invented. The descriptions and the stat numbers come from RebirthItemTracker under a licence that permits it — see NOTICE.md — and the hand-written verdicts are opinions about those facts, not claims about mechanics.

            Casual and Advanced

            Everything above describes Casual: an item is worth what its five axes say, and those axes were assigned — by hand for 181 items, and from tags and quality for the rest. It is a model of the game, and its numbers are arguments rather than facts.

            Advanced stops arguing and runs the game’s own arithmetic. An item contributes the stat deltas the game actually publishes, and those are folded by the formulas the game itself uses:

            QuantityFormula
            Damagebase × √(ups × 1.2 + 1)
            Tear delay16 − 6√(1.3 × tears + 1), floored, never below 5
            Fire rate30 / (delay + 1) — the game runs at 30 frames a second
            DPSdamage × fire rate

            Both square roots are the real reason a tenth damage up is worth a fraction of the first, and they are why the soft cap in the Casual model exists at all — it was an approximation of something concrete. The check that these are the game’s formulas and not merely plausible ones: Isaac holding nothing comes out at 3.5 damage, 10 frames between tears and 2.73 tears a second, which is what his character sheet says.

            Advanced also lets you pick a character, and measures every one of them against Isaac rather than against themselves — otherwise Judas’ damage multiplier would cancel itself out and the choice would be worth nothing.

            Where the stat data and the mechanic tags both come up empty, a third source fills in: 59 items were hand-rated for Casual but publish no stat delta and carry no mechanic — Box of Friends, Book of Revelations, BFFS. A hand rating is already a considered judgement of what an item does in a fight, so Advanced uses it rather than pretending the item is not there. It applies only where nothing else describes the item, because anything with both a rating and a stat delta would otherwise be counted twice.

            Two honest limits. Advanced still offers a smaller pool, because an item that none of the three sources can describe is a pick worth literally nothing, and offering it would be worse than leaving it out. And aoe and tracking still come from the tag table in both modes: piercing and homing are behaviour rather than numbers, and the game states no value for them. Stats where the game gives stats, a model where it gives behaviour.

            The two modes end in the same place — five axes, thirteen fights — so they share the boss model. They do not share its two solved parameters: a different distribution of drafts needs its own difficulty, or one of the modes sits off target.

            Transformations, and what they are worth

            Three items from one family transform Isaac. The families come from the game’s own item metadata rather than a list typed here, which means two real transformations — Bob and Leviathan — can never complete: the data carries one bob item and two devil items.

            A transformation is expensive in a way a synergy is not. It costs three of five picks, and family membership does not correlate with power, so the three items are usually not the three you would otherwise take. At the game’s own payouts that made transforming strictly worse than ignoring transformations: the best build that transformed came in 5.8 points below the best build overall. A mechanic that is never correct is not a decision. So the payouts here are scaled until those two ceilings meet — the best transformed build and the best untransformed build now land within a fraction of a point of each other. Multiplicative axes scale their distance from 1; tracking and evasion move a share of the headroom they have left and stop short of a perfect axis, because multiplying a bounded axis just saturates it.

            Sizing the payout is only half of it. A roll draws from one Pool × Quality cell out of roughly a hundred, and a family’s items are scattered across a handful of them, so a player deliberately chasing a family used to finish it about 4% of the time. That is a lottery, not a plan. Once you are holding two of a family you have spent a pick you would not otherwise have spent, and the run answers it: the roll leans toward a cell where the third item lives, and that item is guaranteed a seat among the six. The page says so on the roll rather than quietly weighting the dice. Two of a family is almost never an accident, so a player chasing nothing meets this on about a fifth of one roll per run. Chasing now completes about 24% of the time. It still costs median score against simply taking the best item every round — it should, since you are passing on better items — but it pays at the top, which is where a 13-0 is decided.

            Where a run actually ends

            The ladder on a finished run tracks how many of a hundred runs are still alive at each fight, so its last value is the headline number. Underneath it sits a sentence naming the fight that ends the most runs and the axis that fight leans on hardest where the build is thinnest.

            Two things about that sentence are deliberate. The fight it names is the one that ends the most runs, which is usually not the one you are least likely to clear — a coin flip on the last fight costs fewer runs than a small risk taken early, and the early one is the one worth knowing about. And the axis is measured against the build’s own strongest axis rather than against some ideal, which keeps the claim modest and true: it says this fight leans on tracking and tracking is where you are thinnest, not that it knows what a perfect build against that boss looks like. When a build is even across the board it says so rather than inventing a culprit.

            Turning a build into odds

            Each fight carries a weight vector over the five axes. Item ratings never encode boss-specific knowledge — the boss weights do all of that.

            The multiplicative axes go through log() so that neutral sits at zero and a halving mirrors a doubling. Tracking and evasion are already bounded 0–1 and are used directly. A fight's score is the weighted sum, and the chance of clearing it is sigmoid(slope × (score − threshold)). The run is the product of all thirteen.

            The thirteen fights are an interpretation. The source schema names Delirium and The Beast alongside a generic “bosses 1–12” ramp, which totals fourteen fights for a thing called 13-0. This build reads it as eleven floor bosses, then Delirium, then The Beast. If that reading is wrong, the weights below are the only thing that needs changing.
            #Fightoffaoetrkdefevathreshold

            Calibration

            slope and difficulty are not hand-picked constants. They are solved by bisection against two targets drawn from the schema, over a Monte Carlo of random five-item drafts:

            • the median draft should land at a 2–8% chance — higher and the achievement is worthless, lower and nobody finishes a run
            • the top 1% of drafts should read above 40% — the "I got a god roll" feeling has to be real

            The solver fits on one seed; tools/validate.mjs re-checks on a different one, so the fit has to generalise rather than memorise its own sample.

            What this is not

            Hitting a target distribution is not the same as being right. Every rating here was assigned by hand and calibrated so that random drafts land where the schema says they should — but no rating has been checked against how the game actually plays. Treat the number as a well-argued opinion with arithmetic on top, not as a measurement.

            The ratings live one-per-line in data/ratings.psv precisely so that disagreeing with one is a one-line diff. If a number looks wrong to you, it might be.

            Ratings are one person's opinion, argued in public. Everything else is arithmetic.
